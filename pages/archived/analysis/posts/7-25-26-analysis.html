Kind Dedekind

Reccomended listening: Chopin "Revolutionary" Etude - Evgeny Kissin

Reading the first chapter of Baby Rudin reminds me of a quote by the German mathematitcian Leopold Kronecker:

"Die ganzen Zahlen hat der liebe Gott gemacht, alles andere ist Menschenwerk."

or, in English:

"God made the integers, all else is the work of man."

I have spent the last five days wondering one thing: what is a gap?
We all know that \(\mathbb{R}\) is gapless, but what does that mean?
It definitely does not mean dense, because \(\mathbb{Q}\) is dense and there are "gaps" in \(\mathbb{Q}\).

When first learning analysis we hear of the least upper bound property (LUP):
All bounded subsets of \(A\) have a least upper bound in \(A\).

Using \(\mathbb{R}\) as \(A\), we are told that this is something called the "axiom of completeness",
and that this axiom means that \(\mathbb{R}\) is "complete" and has "no gaps".

We are then given an example of how \(\mathbb{Q}\) has a gap at \(\sqrt{2}\), and how we can create a subset of \(Q\)
with \(\sqrt{2}\) as the supremum.

For most of us the explaination soon transitions to other topics, but I was not (and still am not quite) satisfied.

LUP = Completeness = "no gaps"?
This seems on its face completely arbitrary to me.

It wasn't until I stumbled across a particular characterization that I could begin to grasp the reals:
\(\mathbb{R}\) is the set of possible suprema for nonempty, bounded subsets of \(\mathbb{Q}\).

This makes sense, as any real is equivalent to a convergent infinite series of rationals.
The elements in the sqeuence of partial sums related to this series is a nonempty, bounded subset of \(\mathbb{Q}\) that has
the associated element in \(\mathbb{R}\) as the suprema.

Additionally, the sequences creatable from any nonempty bounded subset of \(\mathbb{Q}\) can be
interpreted as sequences of partial sums that converge to an element in \(\mathbb{R}\).

This unrigorous Dedekind-like understanding of the reals is, as of yet, my best understanding.

I can well understand that an ordered field \(\mathbb{R}\) with the LUP and \(\mathbb{Q}\) as a subfield
exists (the proof of which is on page 17 of Baby Rudin and is far better presented there than anything I can do),
but I cannot as of yet quite confidently say why the LUP is desireable to begin with.

I have so far spent over 100 hours and done no "math" to try to build an intuition of infinity.
Clearly, I have failed - but failed well enough.

I write this now to Dedekind - thank you for illiminating a path to a solution of a problem that I wouldn't have known about.
It has been fun.
